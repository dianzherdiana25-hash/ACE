## Chapter 1 — The Will, on the Seventh Night

---

The seventh-night prayer gathering ended at nine. The neighbors went home carrying the blessed food parcels in plastic bags, and Ratna's parents' house settled back into its small sounds: dirty glasses soaking in the kitchen basin, mats being rolled up, and on the living-room wall her father's photograph — printed in a hurry a week ago from his ID-card photo, enlarged until it went grainy — looking down like a man who had just realized he no longer had to answer anything.

In the kitchen, a basin of untouched food parcels still waited to be put away. Bu Tini — the vegetable seller from the market, her father's oldest customer — had been the last to say goodbye, and before leaving she had taken Ratna's hand in both of her calloused ones and said, half whispering, as if afraid of being heard by someone who had already gone: "When will the stall open again, Nduk? The market's so quiet without your father's stove. That's where we all have our breakfast. Your father — if I came late, my bowl was always kept aside for me." At the time, all Ratna could answer was "Please pray for us, Bu." Now, washing glasses in the silent kitchen, she found herself thinking about that simple question far longer than about any question of inheritance: for seven days the stall had been locked, and there were people whose breakfast had disappeared, and they asked no one about it except this grieving family.

Ratna washed the glasses with hands that still trembled faintly. She had not slept properly in seven nights. Tomorrow afternoon she was supposed to take the bus back to the city — only two days of her bereavement leave remained, and her manager had already sent a message, polite and clear: *our condolences, but the monthly report cannot wait.*

From the living room, Baskoro called. "Rat. Come here a moment. Pak Haji is here."

Pak Haji Mahmud sat in the chair her father usually sat in. On his lap was a clear plastic folder holding several sheets of paper. Pak Darsim sat at the edge of the mat, near the kitchen door, hands folded, eyes lowered — the posture of a man who already knew what was in this room before the room was opened.

"We'll begin," said Pak Haji Mahmud. "This is Pak Haji Suyatno's own handwritten will. It bears the official stamp. He entrusted it to me two months ago, to be kept in the mosque drawer, with instructions that it be read on the seventh night, in the presence of his four children and Pak Darsim."

Gilang, who had been scrolling his phone with the screen dimmed, put it away in his pocket. Sari sat leaning against the wall, hugging her knees. Ratna dried her hands on a cloth and sat beside her.

Pak Haji read in his usual voice — the voice of an imam who is never in a hurry.

The will was short. What it contained was not.

The first sentence of the letter was not a legal sentence. It was her father's voice, exactly as he sounded when he talked while stirring the broth and looking at no one: *If this letter is being read, it means Bapak can no longer call you home in the usual way. You are not bad children. You are only busy, and busy, if left alone, slowly starts to sound the same as forgetting. So Bapak is using an unusual way.*

Ratna felt Sari, beside her, stop breathing for a moment. Gilang bowed his head. Even Baskoro did not interrupt.

*This house, that stall, those savings — Bapak built all of it bowl by bowl. Not one bit of it came from land that fell into our hands by itself. That is why Bapak does not want it all turned into numbers in a single night, divided, finished, and then the four of you go back to your own cities and meet again at the next funeral. Bapak is old. Bapak knows the next funeral is not far behind.*

Pak Haji Mahmud cleared his throat softly — not because of the sentence, but because he himself had to hold something back — then moved on to the instructions, which her father had written in short sentences like a shopping list:

First: the house, the savings, and the stall are not to be divided now. Second — and here Pak Haji paused, like a man who knows the next sentence will change the room — her father's soto stall must be run by his four children **together, for forty consecutive days**, starting tomorrow morning, opening as usual, before any division may even be discussed.

"Forty days," Baskoro repeated. "Pak Haji, I'm a civil servant. My office is in the next regency. I can't work a food stall for forty days."

"We are not finished, Mas Baskoro." Pak Haji went on. Third: together means every child is present and working at the stall **every day**, and it is recorded. Shifts may be arranged among themselves, taking turns is allowed, as long as not one day passes with a child absent without word the others have agreed to. If a single day fails — the count goes back to zero. Fourth: during those forty days Pak Darsim remains employed as head cook and is paid from the stall's takings as usual; no one may dismiss him, and nothing belonging to the stall may be sold.

"And if we refuse?" Baskoro asked.

Pak Haji Mahmud looked at him steadily, then read the final clause more slowly. Fifth: if those forty days stop halfway, the kiosk and the stall's land are given as *wakaf* — endowed in perpetuity — to the mosque foundation, to be managed for the public good, and the division of the rest of the inheritance stays locked for one full year.

The room went quiet. The old fan sounded like something being forced to work.

"Father planned this," Sari said softly — not a question. "Two months ago he kept going to the mosque in the afternoons. He said he was studying. He was writing this."

"Father didn't trust us to get along without being forced," said Gilang, half laughing, and his laugh died on its own because no one answered it.

Baskoro stood up. "This isn't fair. I'm the eldest, I'm the one farthest away, I'm the—" He stopped, searching for a word that would not offend a dead man, and didn't find one. "The savings, the house — those are our rights too. How can they be held hostage by a soto stall? The price of that kiosk land now—"

"That is precisely why Bapak wrote it that way," Pak Darsim cut in from the edge of the mat, and every head turned, because in seven days of mourning the man had hardly ever spoken more than two sentences. "Forgive me, Mas. If this stall could be divided right away, what would be divided isn't the stall. It's the land."

No one argued. Ratna looked at her brother and saw, for the first time since the funeral, that Baskoro was not angry at the will — he was afraid of his own numbers.

Pak Haji Mahmud folded the will back up. "Tomorrow the stall opens at half past five as usual. Pak Darsim will be there at four to light the stove. The rest is the four of you to decide. I only read it."

After Pak Haji had gone home, Pak Darsim placed a book in Ratna's hands. A thick cloth-covered notebook, its color faded, with her father's handwriting on the first page: *Recipes & Notes — since 1987.*

"This book was always at the stall," Darsim said. "Bapak asked that tonight it be handed to the family."

Ratna turned page after page under the living-room lamp. Broth measures from 1987, the price of chicken from decade to decade, small notes in the margins — *if it rains, a little less salt; people eat more slowly* — the handwriting she had known since childhood, the hand that used to help her with her homework from behind the cashier's table. Her hand stopped near the end of the book.

Its last page was gone.

The tear was neat — too neat to be called damage. Someone had torn one full page out of the book, very close to the spine, and left the page after it blank.

"Pak Darsim." Ratna held the book up. "Where is the last page?"

The old man looked at the torn edge for a moment, with a face that gave nothing away. "That page was never there, Mbak Ratna," he said. "That page's business — your father arranged it himself."

After that he took his leave, carrying home the leftover food parcels for his grandchild, and walked on foot down the dark lane toward the market — toward the stall that tomorrow morning, for the first time in seven days, would light its stove again.

What remained in the living room were four siblings and one problem that could not wait until tomorrow: how four grown people with four lives already running separately could be present at the same stall, every day, for forty days.

"I can do mornings," Sari said first. "I teach from nine. From dawn until half past eight I can be at the stall. But afternoons I can't promise — there are extra lessons."

"I'm flexible," said Gilang. "The app work can happen anytime. Consider me the reserve shift who's always around. Plus content, too — the stall reopening after a week closed, that's good material, Lang—" He glanced at Baskoro and canceled his cheerful tone. "Family documentation, I mean."

Baskoro let out a long breath, like a man doing arithmetic with great reluctance. "My office starts at eight. Forty minutes' travel. I can be at the stall from five to seven, every day, before I leave. That's what makes sense. Saturdays the office is closed, I can stay longer. Sundays…" He rubbed his temple. "Sundays I can too. Done. Forty days."

Then all three of them looked at Ratna, and Ratna felt the whole will suddenly find its final address: herself.

Her bereavement leave ran out the day after tomorrow. Her office was in the big city, five hours from this house. There was no stall shift that could be filled from five hours away.

"I have to talk to the office," she said at last. "Extended leave. Or unpaid leave. A little more than forty days." She heard her own voice say the number and her stomach tightened: her savings would cover two months without pay, no more. "If they don't allow it…" She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't need to. Everyone in that room knew the will's fifth clause as well as she did: if one person stopped, the kiosk and the stall land would belong to the mosque foundation, and their father — who had built that stall from a pushcart in 1987, who never took a single day off even while he was ill — would lose the place forever, by his own children's hands.

"You're the best cook among us," Sari said quietly, as if apologizing for the fact. "Back then, when Father was sick, you were the one he told to take over the stove."

It was true, and Ratna hated that it was true. At fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, she had stood beside her father every school holiday, memorizing the sound of oil that was hot enough, memorizing the faces that arrived before the market stirred. Then she left — a scholarship, the big city, an office job with a name card — and for fifteen years after that she came home twice a year and ate her father's soto as a guest.

Tomorrow she would stand in that stall again. Not as a guest.

"All right," she said. "I'll sort the office out tonight. Tomorrow at four I'm at the stall." She looked at her brother. "Five, Mas. Not six. The stove lights at four, the first customers come at half past five — that's what Pak Darsim has always said."

"Five," said Baskoro, in the tone of a man signing something. "Forty days. After that we divide, we're done, and no one arranges my life from inside a grave ever again."

Gilang raised his phone. "Make a group chat, yeah? What's it called? 'Stall Shifts'? '40 Days'?"

"Its name is 'Pak Yatno's Kids,'" said Sari, and for the first time that night, the room laughed — a small laugh, a tired one, but a laugh.

Late that night, after every room had gone dark, Ratna sat alone at the dining table with the recipe book open in front of her. She turned through its last pages again: price notes, small debts crossed out as paid, then a blank page, then that neat tear. Under the kitchen light she tilted the book and looked at what remained of the tear up close — the scrap of paper left in the spine was too short to read, only the ends of letters, cut in half.

*…for you all…* — no. She couldn't read it. Perhaps there was nothing there that could be read.

She remembered her father's last message the last time they spoke by phone, three weeks ago, which at the time had sounded like any father's message: *When you come home, don't just bring souvenirs. Bring time.* She had answered it with an easy promise: *Of course, Pak. At Eid.* Eid was still four months away. Her father, it turned out, had already known he would not reach that Eid — and had arranged another way to ask her home.

"You win, Pak," Ratna whispered to the book.

She closed it, tied it back up with the raffia string the way her father always had, and set her alarm for half past three in the morning.

---

In the locked stall, after the lane had truly gone quiet, Pak Darsim opened the cashier's drawer with the small key that always hung from a cord around his neck. He did not turn on the light. Inside the drawer, beneath the filled-up cash book, lay folded a brown envelope he had never told anyone about — not Pak Haji, not the four children, not anyone still living.

He looked at the envelope for a very long time, then closed the drawer again, and locked it.

"Tomorrow," he said to the empty stall. "Let's see your children tomorrow."
